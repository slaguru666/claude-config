# Writing scenarios a human can read

Read this before writing or revising any scenario document. It overrides habit.

**The test it has to pass:** a GM reads this in thirty-second glances while four people
wait. Everything below follows from that one sentence.

---

## 1. Open with the shape, before anything else

The first page is a map of the document, not the start of it. In order:

1. **One line of pitch.** What the case is.
2. **A contents list.** Section names. Nothing clever.
3. **The acts, one short paragraph each — 50 to 60 words.** What the act *does*: where
   they are, what they are trying to find out, what they leave with.
4. **The clock**, if there is one, as a single line.

Nothing else goes above this. Not the version table, not the content warnings, not the
design intent. Those go at the back.

**A GM who reads only the first page should be able to describe the evening to a friend.**

---

## 2. Name things. Do not code them

Write **the drift graph**, not FP-H02. Write **the case brief**, not FP-H01.

Codes are for the filing system. They belong in one place — the handout list — written
once, in brackets, after the name. Everywhere else in the document, use the name.

A sentence like *"deal FP-H01, then FP-H02, then FP-H03 and FP-H04 together"* asks the
reader to hold four meaningless tokens in their head. *"Give them the case brief, then
the graph, then the two sheets about the other systems"* asks them to hold nothing.

---

## 3. Bold the label, not the sentence

One bold phrase per block, at the front, naming the thing. Then plain sentences.

> **The wall.** A thousand miles of it standing out of the plain, with no top the eye
> can find. Give it five minutes and do not roll anything.

Never bold inside a sentence to add emphasis. **If every third phrase is bold, nothing
is.** This is the single most common fault and the one that makes a page tiring to look
at before it has been read.

---

## 4. Say who each part is for

State it plainly, the way Classic Traveller does: *this section is for the referee
alone; these two pages may be read by the players.*

A GM needs to know what they can hand across the table without thinking about it.

---

## 5. Short paragraphs, one idea each

Two to four sentences. If a paragraph contains two ideas, it is two paragraphs.

If a sentence has three clauses and two dashes, it is three sentences.

---

## 6. Cut the words that are notes to yourself

These are scaffolding from drafting and they do not belong in the finished document:

| Do not write | Write instead |
|---|---|
| "What it is for:" | A heading that says what it is |
| "The body of the act" | Nothing. Just start the act |
| "This is load-bearing" | Nothing, or say what breaks |
| "The register" / "the conceit" | Nothing. Show it |
| "Do not let the table…" | "If they do X, Y happens" |

**If a line explains the document rather than the game, cut it.**

---

## 7. Give the GM a picture of the shape

One diagram, early: acts across the top, scenes beneath, arrows for what unlocks what,
and the clock down one side. A flowchart or a corkboard — something that can be taken in
at a glance and does not have to be read.

Put it where it prints: in the handout pack or as a plate, not as ASCII in the prose.

---

## 8. Tables for anything with more than two cases

Options, outcomes, reveal orders, what-they-try. A table is read in one look; the same
content as prose is read three times.

Keep cells to one line where you can.

---

## 9. Then cut it in half

Write it, then take out half the words. The scenario is not improved by the sentence
that qualifies the previous sentence.

**Target lengths:** a four-hour convention one-shot is 8 to 14 printed pages, not 23.

---

## The order a finished scenario goes in

1. Pitch, contents, the acts in brief, the clock
2. The diagram
3. What the GM needs to know that the players must not
4. The acts, in order
5. The people
6. The handouts, with their codes
7. Everything about the document itself — version, warnings, design notes, open
   questions — at the **back**

---

## Why this exists

Written 4 November 2026, after FIXED POINT v1.0 came back with the note that it was
hard to read: too many words, too much code, unclear house jargon ("the body of the
act"), and no way to see the shape of the evening without reading all of it.

The references that fixed it: **Classic Traveller** adventures, which state who each
page is for and almost never bold; **Forgotten Lands**, which bolds the label and then
writes plainly; and the **Dee Sanction primer**, which fits a whole game on two pages
using numbered lists and white space.
